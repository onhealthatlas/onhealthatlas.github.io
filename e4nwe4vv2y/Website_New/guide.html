How to use the maps

A short guide to finding your community, reading what you see and understanding where the numbers come from.

  3. Types of areas

Before you start

The maps work on computers, tablets and phones, in current versions of Chrome, Edge, Firefox and Safari. Nothing needs to be installed and you don’t need an account.

Tips for phones and tablets

  • Move the map with two fingers. On most maps, one finger scrolls the page, so you can always scroll past the map. Pinch with two fingers to zoom.
  • Tap an area to see its numbers. On a phone, the details appear just below the map.
  • Search instead of scrolling. Type your riding or community in the search box above the map to go straight to it.
  • Want more room? Turn your phone sideways, or use “Open map in a new tab” to see the map on its own.

Reading a map

Each map is a little different, but most work the same way.

  1. Find your area. Drag the map to move around, or type a place in the search box. To zoom, use the + and − buttons, pinch with two fingers, or hold Ctrl (⌘ on a Mac) while you scroll.
  2. Click or tap an area. A box opens with the numbers for that place, such as the average rent or the share of people registered with a family doctor.
  3. Change what’s shown. Many maps let you switch between measures or years. Look for the buttons above the map or in one of its corners.
  4. Check the legend. The legend explains what each colour or symbol means. On most maps, darker colours mean higher values.
  5. Read the details below the map. Each map page lists what the map shows, the type of area, the time period and the source. The “About this map” link above the map takes you straight there.

Types of areas

Data is published for different kinds of areas, so the maps don’t all use the same boundaries. We use plain names on this site; the technical names are shown in brackets.

Municipality (census subdivision, CSD)
A city, town, township or First Nation reserve, as defined by Statistics Canada for the census.
Metro area (census metropolitan area, CMA)
A large city and the communities around it, with at least 100,000 people in total and 50,000 in the core. Toronto, Ottawa and Hamilton are examples.
Urban area (census agglomeration, CA)
A smaller city and its surrounding area, with at least 10,000 people in the core. Examples include Timmins, Cornwall and Owen Sound.
Postal area (forward sortation area, FSA)
The area covered by the first three characters of a postal code, like M3C.
Riding (provincial electoral district)
An area that elects one Member of Provincial Parliament (MPP) to Queen’s Park. Ontario has 124 ridings.
Neighbourhood zone (Rental Market Survey zone)
A neighbourhood-sized area that Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation uses to report rents within a city.

Key terms

Registered (rostered) patient
A patient formally enrolled with a family doctor or primary care team, who is responsible for their ongoing care.
Regular family doctor
Someone has a regular family doctor if health records show a family doctor, nurse practitioner or clinic they regularly get their primary care from. Researchers describe people without one as “uncertainly attached”.
Core housing need
A household is in core housing need if its home costs 30% or more of its before-tax income, is too crowded for the household, or needs major repairs, and it would also have to spend 30% or more of its income to rent an acceptable home nearby.
Vacancy rate
The share of rental apartments that are empty and available to rent right away when surveyed. A low rate means renters have few options and landlords can charge more.
Turnover rate
The share of rental units where a new tenant moved in during the past year.
Average and median rent
The average adds up all rents and divides by the number of units. The median is the middle value: half of units rent for less and half for more. The median is less affected by a few very expensive units.
Housing start
A new home counts as started when work begins on its foundation. In an apartment building, every unit counts as started at the same time.
Completion
A new home counts as completed when all, or nearly all, of the planned construction work is done.
Rent-geared-to-income (RGI)
Rent set as a share of a household’s income, usually 30%, instead of at market rates. Common in community housing.

Where the data comes from

Every map uses publicly available data. The main sources are:

  • Canada Mortgage and Housing Corporation (CMHC), the federal housing agency, for rents, vacancy, turnover, seniors’ housing, new construction, new home prices and sales, and mortgages.
  • Statistics Canada for core housing need and for the boundaries of municipalities, metro areas and urban areas.
  • Provincial health data for the family doctor maps: the Ontario Rostering Physician and Patient Counts Report, and figures published by INSPIRE-PHC, a primary care research program.

Each map page lists its exact source. Most of the underlying data can be downloaded for free from CMHC’s Housing Market Information Portal and from Statistics Canada.

Why some areas are blank

Agencies don’t publish every number for every place. Some areas are too small to survey reliably, and some values are withheld to protect privacy. When that happens, the area appears without data.

Why the years differ

Each source publishes on its own schedule. Every map uses the most recent data that was available when it was made, and the time period is listed on the map page.

Questions

Is this a government website?

No. Ontario Reality Check is produced by the office of Dr. Adil Shamji, MPP for Don Valley East. It is not a Government of Ontario website. The data it uses comes from public agencies.

Can I share these maps?

Yes. Share links to any map freely. If you use a screenshot, please credit Ontario Reality Check and the original data source listed on the map page.

My community isn’t on a map. Why not?

Some maps only cover places above a certain population, because that’s all the source reports. Others leave out areas where data was withheld. The area type and population limit are listed on each map page.

I think I found a mistake.

Please let us know. Contact Dr. Shamji’s office through adilshamji.ca and tell us which map and which area.

A map won’t load.

Try refreshing the page, or open the map in its own tab using the button above the map. If it still doesn’t load, try a different browser or a computer.
